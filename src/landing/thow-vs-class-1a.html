Things to know

Tiny house on wheels, or Class 1a modular home?

They can look almost identical parked side by side. One of them is a house. The other is legally a vehicle — and that single distinction drives nearly every practical difference between them.

It's the question we're asked most often, usually in the first phone call. Most people have worked out that one of them can be moved. What's less obvious is how far that difference reaches — into what you're allowed to build, how long it takes, whether a bank will lend against it, what your insurer will cover, and what happens to the money when you sell.

Here's the whole picture in plain terms. This is general information for Australian buyers rather than guidance for any one council, because local rules vary enormously and yours will have the final say.

The short version

A tiny house on wheels keeps its trailer, so the law treats it as a caravan. A Class 1a modular home is craned onto permanent footings, so the law treats it as a house. Everything below follows from that.

Tiny house on wheels

Classified as a caravan

A dwelling built on a purpose-built, road-legal trailer. Because it keeps its wheels and chassis, it's regulated as a registrable vehicle rather than as a building.

There's no building approval process to go through — and in most places, no way to have it approved as a permanent dwelling either. What you get instead is speed and freedom of movement.

Choose it for flexibility. Don't choose it expecting a permanent, approved address.

Class 1a modular home

A house, built off site

A standard dwelling under the National Construction Code, manufactured in a factory and craned onto permanent footings. Class 1a covers detached houses and attached dwellings like townhouses.

It's designed, certified and inspected to exactly the same standards as anything built on site, and it goes through the same approvals. The construction method is different; the legal status isn't.

Choose it for permanence. Expect an approvals process and a timeline to match.

The comparison in detail

Rules differ between states, territories and local governments, so read the entries below as the general position rather than a guarantee for your address.

If you go modular: what approval actually involves

The sequence varies by state and council, and several steps run in parallel, but the shape of it is consistent across the country.

  1. Confirm what your land allowsZoning, minimum lot size, setbacks, height and site cover, plus any overlay — bushfire, flood, character, vegetation, heritage. This determines whether a second dwelling is possible at all.
  2. Planning approval, if requiredSome proposals are accepted development and need no planning application. Others require one, and that's the step most likely to add months.
  3. Site investigation and designSoil classification, footing design, wind and bushfire ratings, energy efficiency assessment and stormwater. These feed the working drawings.
  4. Building approvalIssued by a private certifier or the council. Plumbing and drainage approval is usually a separate application.
  5. Manufacture and site worksThe home is built in the factory while footings, services and access are prepared on site. This overlap is where modular buys back time.
  6. Delivery, crane and connectionModules are transported, craned onto the footings, fixed down, joined and connected to services.
  7. Final inspection and certificateThe certifier inspects and issues the final certificate. Only at that point is the home lawfully occupiable.

The bottleneck is rarely the house. It's approvals, service connections and site access. Start those conversations before you commit to a design.

If you go with a THOW: where it sits legally

A tiny house on wheels isn't a loophole into an approved dwelling, and it's worth being clear-eyed about that before you buy.

Because it's a vehicle, no building approval applies to it and none can be obtained for it. What governs it instead is your local government's rules about occupying a caravan on private land — and those rules are genuinely local. Some councils allow occupation for a set number of days each year. Some allow it only for visitors. Some require a permit. Some prohibit it outright. A handful have started writing tiny homes into their planning schemes explicitly.

The consequences are worth understanding. A THOW generally can't be counted as a secondary dwelling, won't satisfy a condition requiring an approved dwelling on the land, and can't be financed or insured as a house. Its value sits with you rather than with the land.

None of that makes it the wrong choice. It makes it a different product — suited to people who value mobility, speed and independence from the approvals system over permanence.

Which one fits your situation

A THOW tends to suit you if

  • You don't own the land, or don't intend to keep it long term
  • You may need to relocate the home within a few years
  • The site has no sewer, or connecting services would be costly
  • You need somewhere to live quickly and can't wait on approvals
  • Access is tight and craning would be difficult or expensive
  • You're comfortable working within your council's rules on occupying a caravan

A modular home tends to suit you if

  • This is a permanent home, a rental, or accommodation for family
  • You want the build to add value to the property
  • You need mortgage or construction finance
  • You want a lawful, certified dwelling with warranty protection
  • You need more space than towing dimensions allow
  • The site has, or can reasonably get, permanent services and crane access

Questions worth asking your council

One phone call to your local planning counter usually resolves more than a week of reading. These are the questions that matter most.

  • Is a secondary dwelling permitted on my lot, and what minimum lot size applies?
  • Does my proposal need planning approval, or is it accepted development?
  • Which overlays apply to my property, and what do they require?
  • What setbacks, height limits and site cover apply?
  • Are there additional parking requirements for a second dwelling?
  • Can the site connect to sewer, or is on-site treatment required?
  • What infrastructure charges apply to a new dwelling?
  • What are the local rules for occupying a caravan on private land?
  • Is a permit needed for that, and are there time limits?
  • Does the scheme mention tiny homes or movable dwellings specifically?
  • Are there restrictions on renting out a second dwelling separately?
  • Is vehicle and crane access to the rear of the property achievable?
